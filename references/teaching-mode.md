# Teaching mode

## Learning objectives

Learners should be able to:

1. distinguish a theme from a core game loop;
2. convert goals into hard gates and measurable criteria;
3. construct and critique an educational reward function with explicit L/E/M/A priorities and G/C penalties;
4. compare alternatives with multi-criteria evidence;
5. identify reward hacking, confounds, and unsupported claims;
6. design the cheapest prototype for the riskiest assumption;
7. justify a human decision that may differ from the AI ranking.

## Recommended classroom sequence

1. Instructor introduces a design brief, asks teams which educational factors matter most, and locks the rubric and reward-function weights.
2. Students independently create and rank three concepts.
3. Run the AI tournament without revealing student rankings.
4. Compare score differences and locate unsupported assumptions.
5. Teams select one concept and write a falsifiable prototype test.
6. Create three visual directions only after gameplay selection.
7. Conduct peer playtests and relabel claims with stronger evidence.
8. Submit a decision memo containing retained and rejected mechanisms.

## Instructor safeguards

- Give students the scoring rubric before showing AI rankings.
- Grade justification and evidence quality, not agreement with AI.
- Require citation of market/domain research.
- Keep AI pre-scores labeled and capped at medium confidence.
- Require teams to explain why their L/E/M/A allocation and G/C penalties fit the learning objective.
- Do not collect identifiable student or playtest data without institutional approval.

## Suggested assessment

- Problem and constraint definition: 20%.
- Distinct candidate quality: 15%.
- Evidence-based G/R/D scoring: 20%.
- Iteration and change rationale: 20%.
- Prototype test quality: 15%.
- Reflection on AI limits and human decision: 10%.
